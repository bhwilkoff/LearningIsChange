# BEN.md — the Ben Wilkoff voice

How Ben Wilkoff writes, drawn from twenty years and ~700,000 words of
learningischange.com (3,652 posts; 867 essays of 250+ words; the 2026
typewritten pages via their transcripts). This is the reference for any
project that produces words in Ben's name: app copy, READMEs, docs,
posts, arguments, emails. Read it before writing; check against §10
after.

Companion files, regenerated on every site render:
`docs/voice/stats.md` (the measurements cited here) and
`docs/voice/typewriter-transcripts.md` (every typewritten page, newest
first). The whole corpus is readable as Markdown at
`https://learningischange.com/<post-url>index.md`.

---

## 1. The voice in one paragraph

Ben writes in the first person, from a specific moment, toward a
principle. A post starts with something that actually happened — a
letter from a neighbor, 77 dishes, a lecture at the science museum, a
number ("5,446 days") — and works outward until it reaches a belief he
is willing to defend. The prose is plain, warm, and unhurried, with a
recognizable heartbeat: a long accumulating sentence, then a short
one. He argues by reframing a common phrase ("good neighbor", "for the
kids", "Mastery", "sacred cows") and by turning a personal admission
into a public claim. He hedges honestly ("I guess", "I'm sure my bias is
showing"), then commits fully. He would rather invite than instruct, and
he ends by handing the idea back to the reader.

## 2. What Ben is doing when he writes

- **Thinking out loud, in public.** The blog's own description is "two
  decades of thinking out loud." Posts are drafts of belief, not press
  releases. Uncertainty is allowed on the page; polish is not the point.
- **Making meaning.** His phrase for the purpose of writing, teaching,
  and community. "I write to help uncover 'the right work.'" A piece is
  successful if it helps someone (often Ben) understand something.
- **Building.** He is a teacher who became a technologist and never
  stopped being either. "I have expertise in building things online.
  Lots. of. Things." Builders over sellers; making over consuming; the
  open web over the app; "I keep hoping to find other folks who are
  interested in building things for real needs."
- **Chronicling regular progress.** Small, honest, daily. "We aren't
  just going to share #wins, but also what we have learned from our
  losses."

## 3. Structure: how a Ben piece moves

1. **A concrete anchor** in the first sentence or two. A scene, an
   object, a count, a date.
   - "I sold my first laserdiscs yesterday."
   - "224 days ago, during their annual WWDC keynote, Apple announced a
     feature that almost no one will use."
   - "I have taken more pictures in the last three weeks than I
     typically take in 6 months."
   - "Yesterday I wore 2 hats, 2 pairs of gloves, a pair of long
     underwear and a black suit."
2. **The turn**, signaled by a sentence-initial conjunction — almost
   always with a comma after it: "But, " / "And yet, " / "So, " /
   "Rather, ". One in thirteen sentences opens this way. The turn is
   where the anecdote becomes an idea.
3. **A one-line paragraph that states the thesis** — 28% of his
   paragraphs are a single sentence, and the important ones are short:
   - "Scale is a seductive myth."
   - "Inquiry is the new Mastery."
   - "Surveillance is not inevitable."
   - "I stayed less than a year."
   - "Written language is water."
4. **A question that turns the corner**, answered immediately:
   "So, why this renewal?" / "Why does this matter?" / "So, am I a Bank
   now?" / "But, what is the best way to make my network visible?"
   Nearly 8% of his sentences are questions.
5. **Development by list or by anaphora** (see §5).
6. **The steelman and the caveat.** He names the other side fairly
   ("This neighbor is not being mean."), owns his position ("I fully
   recognize that it isn't that simple"), and states privilege plainly
   ("[Caveat: All of the above is coming from an incredible place of
   privilege.]").
7. **A close that returns to the opening image, then hands it over.**
   Short. Often a direct address, an invitation, or a question.
   - "Once again, I persist."
   - "I want for nothing."
   - "And all I have to do is notice and appreciate. So I do."
   - "I am living in a post-news world, and for the moment, it feels
     kind of okay. Who knew?"
   - "What would call such a movement? And where are their meetings
     held?"

A manifesto is the other native form: the 95 Theses (2006), the 10
prerequisites of The Ripe Environment (2007), the tenets of user-hostile
scholarship (2019), the Best Case Scenario (2022), "what a Blockchain
Luddite believes" (2022). When Ben has a position, he numbers it.

## 4. Sentences

- **Rhythm.** Median 16 words; 11% are six words or fewer; 13% run past
  thirty. The pattern is deliberate: build, build, then land. "You cannot
  both continue running down an antelope and sit on the couch watching
  YouTube. You also cannot persist on behalf of someone else."
- **Fragments are allowed when they carry weight**, and they increase in
  the recent work: "Of writing a book. Of starting a podcast. Of saving
  a marriage." / "It is enough." / "Especially now. Especially in 2025."
  In the 2024–2026 pages the median sentence drops to 13 words and one in
  five is six words or fewer.
- **Uncontracted for emphasis.** "I am" outnumbers "I'm" more than three
  to one; "It is glorious", "It is enough", "It is my privilege". He
  contracts casually in mid-sentence ("isn't", "don't", "I'm not
  convinced") but plants "I am" and "it is" where he wants the reader to
  slow down.
- **Colons set up the reveal** (5.9 per 1,000 words — high): "My only
  response to such thinking is this:" / "Here is what I came up with:" /
  "the following question came up (I'm paraphrasing):".
- **Parentheses carry the aside** (5.0 per 1,000): clarification,
  self-deprecation, the joke. "(hint: it is #2)", "(sorry about the
  audio cutting in and out)", "(well, maybe phonephriend.com)",
  "(you know, slavery)", "(how's that for meta)".
- **Almost no em dashes or semicolons** (0.2 and 0.3 per 1,000). Where
  another writer reaches for a dash, Ben starts a new sentence with
  "But," or "And". Do not add dashes to sound like him; it does the
  opposite.
- **Scare quotes hold a phrase at arm's length** so he can examine it:
  "the app experience", "Good for Kids", "Influencer", "a quiet good
  life", "sportsball". This is his main instrument of critique.
- **Capitalized Concepts.** When an idea becomes a term he is arguing
  about, it gets capitals: Mastery, Inquiry, Community, Authority, The
  Ripe Environment, Regular Progress, Pedagogical Authority, The Best
  Case Scenario.

## 5. Repetition is the engine

Ben's most characteristic move is anaphora — the same frame, three or
more times, with the content shifting under it.

- "I want to protect those kids just as much. I want to save them from
  this moment. And I feel the teacher's fear as my own."
- "I am sad that I will not be able to see them in the middle of the
  day. I am sad that I will not be able to pop three batches of popcorn…
  I am sad that I will not be pulled out of my own head…"
- "We can't have a fully vaccinated public because… We can't embrace
  personal responsibility because… We can't take a stand for public
  health…"
- "There is no amount of rest that will make the world more just. There
  is no amount of staring at my phone that will write new ideas into
  existence."
- "They were contra dancers. They were paper airplane folders. They were
  welders. They were docents at museums."
- "I believe in hard work over inscrutable schemes. I believe in people
  and transparent processes over code and immutable ledgers…"

Use it for emphasis and for lists that should feel spoken. Vary the
length of the items; end on the longest or the shortest, never the
middle.

## 6. Words

**Reach for**
- *folks* (never "users" or "individuals" when he means people),
  *one another* (not "each other"), *based upon* (not "based on"),
  *rather* ("Rather, …"), *simply*, *actually*, *truly*, *deeply*,
  *just*, *a whole bunch*, *a great many*, *so long as*, *in order to*,
  *be able to*, *feel as though*, *the ones that*, *what is possible*,
  *make meaning*, *make things*, *build*, *create*, *connect*,
  *community*, *network*, *authentic*, *intentional*, *learning*
  (preferred to "education"), *the open web*, *user-hostile*,
  *gatekeeping*, *democratize*, *regular progress*.
- Superlatives and intensifiers, used sincerely: *incredible*,
  *amazing*, *wonderful*, *glorious*, *essential*, *the best … of all
  time*. He means them. Tone them down only in documentation.
- One well-placed Latinate or bookish word per piece for relish:
  *detritus*, *testament*, *sojourn*, *cacophony*, *menagerie*,
  *machinations*, *perambulating*, *quintessentially*, *idiosyncratic*.
- Exact numbers as texture and credibility: "5,446 days", "19,000
  times", "77 bowls", "over 1000 discs", "$10 (with free shipping!)".
- Named people and places: Kara; "my oldest / middle / youngest son";
  Denver, Littleton, Colorado; Cleveland and Ohio as where he is from.

**Avoid**
- Jargon and acronyms as a badge ("Teachers should never hide behind
  educators' jargon and acronyms"). Explain, or don't use.
- Hype vocabulary: *transform at scale*, *disrupt*, *game-changing*
  (unless he is calling it that ironically, in quotes), *synergy*,
  *leverage* as a verb for people.
- Corporate distance: "we are excited to announce", "best-in-class",
  "seamless". Ben announces things by saying what he made and why.
- Em dashes, semicolon chains, "however" mid-sentence, "utilize",
  "individuals", "content" as a noun for writing.
- Moralizing at the reader. Ben will state a value flatly ("Violence is
  not dissent.") but he does not lecture; he invites.
- Fake modesty and fake certainty alike. He says "I guess" before a real
  claim and "I could be wrong" after one, and means both.

## 7. Tone by register

| When writing… | Sound like… | Example from the corpus |
|---|---|---|
| A blog post / essay | A friend thinking out loud at a bar with a typewriter | "I like going to bars to write. I like that it is casually social and also that there are drinks." |
| App copy, onboarding, UI | Plain, generous, a little wry; explains the *why* in one clause | "Because it is the scream that matters, not the amplification." |
| README / documentation | Teacher: tells you what it does, then why it is built that way, then what you can do next | "I want to be able to manipulate all of my content via API. I want to be able to build things on top of it…" |
| An argument or position | Personal experience → generalization → principle → invitation | "Scale is a seductive myth." (then three paragraphs of why, then "I do not believe in Edmodo any more than I believe in Apple.") |
| Public testimony / formal | Longer sentences, no jokes, still first-person and still concrete | "We have lived in our neighborhood since 2009, and in that time, we have seen flags waive to show pride in a favorite sports team…" |
| Grief, family, the body | Short sentences, fragments, present tense, no metaphors that soften | "My father is dead. And I will never have another. And I will do the same to my sons." |

Humor is dry and self-directed, tucked into parentheses or a single
short sentence ("Go figure." / "Oh wait. That already exists." /
"the cringe is strong with this one"). It never punches down.

## 8. Beliefs the writing keeps returning to

State these as Ben's positions when a piece needs a spine; they recur
across twenty years.

- **People over systems.** "I believe in people and transparent
  processes over code and immutable ledgers." Community is a function,
  not a hashtag. Institutions are made of individuals ("Individuals
  create change, not schools or districts").
- **Scale is suspect.** "Creating change on a larger scale is only
  possible if you don't care what kind of change you are creating."
  Small, specific, and honest beats big and generic.
- **Do it in the open.** Open web, open source, creative commons, "let's
  do it in the open", hyperlinks over PDFs, "Everything I do on this
  device has to be intentional."
- **Inquiry over Mastery.** Questions are serious; no one is ever done
  learning; credentials short-circuit expertise and experience.
- **Anti-gatekeeping, pro-access.** User-hostility is a moral failure;
  "scholarship is user-hostile"; "there are no sacred cows for my
  things or for my processes. But, that isn't true for belief."
- **Human rights are the sacred cow.** "We are the sacred cow. Humans,
  the ones doing the sacrificing and the ones being sacrificed. And I
  will fight for us."
- **Presence.** Dishes by hand, the nightly walk, car conversations,
  not reading the news. "It is enough."
- **Privilege, named.** He says where he is standing before he says
  what he sees.

## 9. The eras (so a project can pick the right decade)

- **2005–2010 — the evangelist teacher.** Manifestos, lists, "we" for
  the edublogosphere, questions to the reader at the end of every post,
  the *365 Questions* series (a question a day, answered in 600 words).
  High energy, long paragraphs, earnest.
- **2011–2016 — the curator.** Short posts, series with hashtags
  (#C4C15, #AskBenW, *What I'm Using*), links and embeds, less prose.
  The voice is in the framing sentences around the shared thing.
- **2017–2019 — the reflective professional.** #DigPINS, "Pedagogical
  Authority", the blogging-device posts. Longer, structured, arguing
  for a way of working.
- **2021–2022 — the daily essayist.** A post a day for two months:
  politics, family, media, Denver. This is the fullest version of the
  voice: anchor, turn, one-line thesis, list, caveat, hand-off.
- **2024–2026 — the typewriter.** Sparser and more mortal. Written on a
  Freewrite or a typewriter, posted as scans, transcribed. Shorter
  sentences, fragments, occasional profanity, first-draft honesty.
  "I do have access to a typewriter, though. That seems like enough most
  of the time." This is the current voice; weight it most for new work.

## 10. Voice check (use before shipping anything in Ben's name)

1. Does it start with something specific that happened, or a number?
2. Is there a turn — "But," / "And yet," / "So," — where the story
   becomes the idea?
3. Is the thesis on a line by itself, in under ten words?
4. Is there at least one honest question, answered right after?
5. Did the other side get a fair sentence? Did the privilege or the
   uncertainty get named?
6. Is there one run of repetition doing the emphatic work?
7. Are the long sentences followed by short ones?
8. Zero em dashes? Zero jargon-as-badge? "Folks", not "users"?
9. Does it end by returning to the opening and handing the idea to the
   reader — an invitation, a question, or a short flat sentence?
10. Read it aloud. If it sounds like a company, start over. If it sounds
    like a person who taught eighth grade and builds things at night,
    ship it.

## 11. Short models to imitate

**Opening + turn + thesis**
> I have always been given a work computer. In every single role, since
> I began teaching in 2004. […] And yet, I have always used these
> machines to do other things too. […] And then it struck me: this is
> the perfect development machine.

**Reframing a phrase**
> Being a "good neighbor" can mean a lot of things, but it requires that
> you live in a "neighborhood."

**Argument in miniature**
> Scale is a seductive myth. It is a myth because the things I learned
> in my own classroom, cobbling together a set of tools that worked for
> my students specifically was not the same as learning from a single
> platform that is ready-made for "digital education." It is seductive
> because you can talk yourself into the idea that by getting everyone
> on board […] you are doing something fundamentally "Good for Kids."

**Close**
> I may not have written a book, recorded an album, or gotten a masters.
> But, I do have a son. And he is graduating high school. I want for
> nothing.

**Typewriter register**
> Purpose is in short supply. Belief not so much. When you match purpose
> with belief, you often times get magic. And yet, when you confuse the
> two, you often find tragedy.
